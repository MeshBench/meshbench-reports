A backoff that knows how crowded it is, and overdoes it

The retransmit window is the same width in a cluster of twelve repeaters as on a hilltop with one neighbour. Widening it per neighbour is a sound idea implemented with a bad constant: on a dense real mesh it produced delays long enough to truncate the flood, costing eighteen points of reach.

Where it comes from

calcRxDelay() knows a packet's score and nothing about how many neighbours are about to make the same decision. Two repeaters and twelve repeaters draw their retransmit delay from the same range, so the more crowded the neighbourhood the more likely two of them land together.

There is no neighbour table in the repeater to consult, but there is one free source of the information: the last hash in a flood packet's path is whoever relayed it to us, which is a neighbour by definition.

The change

Keep an ageing 24 entry table of those hashes with a two minute window, and add one airtime of spread to the retransmit window per neighbour currently in it.

uint32_t span = 5*t + t * (uint32_t)neighbourCount();
return getRNG()->nextInt(0, span + 1);

Cost. A 24 entry table, one pass over it per retransmit, one write per received flood.

What was predicted, before running it

Fewer collisions in dense clusters at the cost of latency in sparse ones. Pre-registered as the idea most likely to help in one place and hurt in another.

What happened

The mechanism works; the constant is wrong

Reach fell from 56.2% to 38.0% and transmissions by 13.8%. Collisions did fall, by 12.2%, which is the intended effect. But the delay grew so large that relays stopped happening inside the run at all.

Why it came out that way

On this network a repeater hears far more than a handful of neighbours. The table fills, neighbourCount() returns something in the twenties, and the retransmit window becomes 5t + 20t: twenty-five airtimes instead of five, a fivefold increase. That is not a spread, it is a stall.

The per-message figures show it is truncation rather than delay. Message 3 reached 234 repeaters and 28 companions in both arms, exactly as the control did. The other three messages stopped early, at 70 and 116 repeaters against 234 and 158. A flood that is merely slowed arrives late; a flood that stops has run out of time. Ninety seconds of simulated time is not long enough for a relay chain where every hop waits twenty-five airtimes.

That the result coincides with idea 3's transmission count and delivered set to the digit is worth stating rather than hiding. The two arms are different binaries, verified by checksum, and their receptions and collisions differ. What they share is where the flood stops: both truncate it at the same depth, and on a fixed topology with a fixed origin the set of nodes reached by a flood truncated at that depth is the same set. It is a coincidence of outcome, not of code, and it is the sort of thing that should make a reader suspicious, so it is said here rather than left to be noticed.

The idea is not refuted. The constant is. One airtime per neighbour is defensible on a mesh where a node has three or four neighbours and indefensible where it has twenty five.

Should it be adopted?

Not at this constant

Not as written. The mechanism is right and the multiplier is wrong: it needs a bound, for example min(neighbourCount(), 4), or a square root, or a widening of the window rather than a multiplication of it. That is a one line change and a rerun, and it is the arm in this series most worth trying again rather than abandoning.

For

  • Collisions fell 12.2%, which is the effect the idea exists to produce.
  • The information it needs is free: the previous hop's hash is already in every flood packet.
  • Cheap: 24 bytes of table, a pass over it per retransmit.
  • It is the only idea in the series that adapts to local conditions rather than applying one constant everywhere, which is the right shape for a protocol that runs on both a village and a city.

Against

  • Eighteen points of reach at the constant tested.
  • The delay scales linearly with neighbours and neighbours scale with density, so the worst behaviour appears exactly where the mesh is busiest.
  • It needs a bound and a rerun before it can be judged on its merits.
  • A node's neighbour count from path hashes is approximate: one byte per hash means distinct neighbours can share an entry, so the count understates density in the very case that matters.

How it was run

The 311 node Scotland and Ireland import, built from live CoreScope with the transport regions the real nodes hold. Both arms run in the same sweep, on the same two seeds, against the same network, with only the repeater firmware differing. Four senders, spread rather than adjacent, so they contend with the mesh rather than with each other. Ninety seconds of simulated time per run.

Node storage is wiped before the sweep. A node keeps its preferences between runs exactly as hardware does, so one that has run before loads its stored value and never reaches a changed default: both arms then return identical numbers and the change looks inert.

The control is MeshCore at the commit repeater-v1.17.0 points at, built by the same script as the arm. It produced 573 transmissions and 4,830 receptions in every sweep of this series, seven sweeps over three hours. That is the reproducibility check, and without it none of the deltas on this page would be attributable to the firmware.

Limits

Two seeds. The plus or minus twenty per cent figure quoted elsewhere for this simulator belongs to a different measurement, reach under contention from around eight simultaneous senders; it is a property of that contention rather than of the simulator, and it is not quoted here as though it were universal. What can be said is that the control repeated exactly, so a difference between arms is the firmware. Two seeds is still a thin basis for a small delta, and anything under a few per cent on this page should be read as unproven rather than as a result.

The simulator is kinder than the air: no multipath, no body loss, no oscillator error, no interference beyond the mesh's own traffic. Every one of those omissions makes real links worse, so the absolute numbers are a best case and the comparison is the finding.

The MeshCore branch carrying this change is local and is not pushed anywhere.
